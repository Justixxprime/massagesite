Why Deep Tissue Sometimes
Hurts the Next Day

Journal Article Written by Amara

If you've had a deep tissue session and woken up the next day feeling like you did a workout you don't remember signing up for, that's normal, and it's worth understanding why.

Deep tissue work applies sustained, targeted pressure to release tension in muscle layers that don't usually get touched in a lighter massage. That pressure creates tiny amounts of inflammation in the tissue, similar to what happens after exercise. The soreness that follows is your body's normal repair response, not a sign that anything went wrong.

What's actually happening in the muscle

Think of a muscle fiber that's been shortened and stuck together for months, the kind of adhesion that builds up from sitting at a desk, favoring one side after an injury, or just repeating the same motion thousands of times. Deep tissue technique works those fibers apart layer by layer, using slow strokes and sustained pressure rather than the faster gliding strokes of a relaxation massage. That mechanical separation is effective, but it's also a genuine physical stressor on tissue that hasn't been worked that directly before.

The result is very similar to delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS) after a hard workout: small-scale, normal inflammation as part of the repair process, not damage. It typically peaks within 24 hours and fades on its own.

What actually helps

Hydration matters more than people expect. Water helps flush the metabolic byproducts released during the session, similar to how it helps after a workout. A warm, not hot, shower or bath in the hours after can also ease lingering tightness, along with light movement like walking rather than sitting still for long stretches.

Gentle stretching of the areas that were worked can help too, but avoid anything strenuous for the rest of that day. Most soreness fades within 24 to 48 hours. If it's still significant after that, or if something felt sharp or wrong during the session itself, that's worth mentioning at your next appointment so pressure can be adjusted going forward.

When to speak up mid-session

Good deep tissue work should feel like firm, effective pressure, not sharp pain. There's a useful mental scale to use during a session: a 6 or 7 out of 10 is normal for deep work, a "hurts so good" kind of intensity. If something crosses from "intense but good" into "make it stop," that's the signal to say something immediately. Pressure is always adjustable in real time, and a good therapist would always rather ease off than push through something that doesn't feel right.

If you're newer to deep tissue work, it's worth mentioning that at booking, so the first session can start a little lighter and build from there rather than going straight to full intensity.
